# MQTT PoC Technical Assessment

## Recommendation

Proceed to a separately approved non-production design/review; do not infer production readiness from this local PoC. The service-isolation design and SDK integration are demonstrated. Topic/filter cardinality is not the principal proxy for the cost of many persistent sessions.

Evidence: [observed benchmark report](mqtt-poc-benchmark.md), [machine-readable results](mqtt-poc-benchmark-results.json), [real-broker isolation tests](../../apps/mqtt-poc/src/integration.ts), [reliability tests](../../apps/mqtt-poc/src/reliability.ts), [standalone DX test](../../apps/mqtt-poc/src/dx-test.ts), and [service/create scenario H](../../apps/mqtt-poc/src/service-scenario.ts). Results use RabbitMQ 4.3.6, verified TLS, QoS1, one classic-queue node on a local Docker VM. No production resources or personal data were used.

## Decision Questions

1. **Can RabbitMQ MQTT dynamically manage thousands of logical topics? Demonstrated locally.** 100/1000/5000/10000 distinct topic names were published with one subscription/session queue. No topic object creation or redeploy was required. This does not establish production capacity or subscriber scalability.
2. **What do thousands of persistent subscriptions cost? Partially demonstrated.** 10000 accepted filters on 10 persistent sessions created 10 queues and delivered 10000 expected copies. Separately, 500 persistent sessions created 500 queues and received 15000 expected copies from 30 publications. Many thousands of persistent queues are unverified. Full onboarding and broker/generator resource snapshots are recorded. Before/after memory includes GC and prior scenario history; it cannot be interpreted as precise bytes/filter or bytes/session. Queue deletion, failed partial provisioning and transient exclusive-queue cleanup were tested; steady-state deletion throughput was not benchmarked.
3. **What limits scalability? Partially measured.** Session queues/backlog, filter/binding cardinality, fanout, individual QoS1 acknowledgements, TLS/connect churn, sockets/FDs, broker CPU/storage and the HTTP authentication service. Closed-loop D achieved about 755 publishes/s and 12500 deliveries from 1500 publications in its short configuration, not a saturation limit. The generator shares the host/VM and management sampling adds overhead. High-cardinality routing and full registration writes must be separated in a larger experiment.
4. **What are the isolation risks? Controls demonstrated, review still required.** A/B/C namespace permissions, session resource ACLs, CONNECT identity/clientId binding, explicit denial of wildcards/PUBLISH/impersonation, and active-connection revocation passed. Missing topic permissions would be permissive in the internal backend; activation therefore waits for verified ACLs. Client-chosen identifiers cannot be trusted by substitution alone. Cache/revocation races, auth-backend availability, compromised issuer/admin identity, public-network abuse and production key validation need review. G reported zero observed cross-service deliveries in three 25-consumer repeats and an active forbidden subscription probe.
5. **How easy is external integration? Demonstrated for TypeScript.** The sample installs outside the monorepo, connects with a supplied CA/bundle using a standard MQTT SDK, subscribes, validates/processes a synthetic event, and shuts down. No broker topology or Management API is needed. Three public dependencies and two file-path settings are used. Stable clientId, certificate trust, rotation/reconnect, bounded offline retention and durable consumer idempotency are meaningful integration obligations. The Java excerpt is unverified, not a second tested SDK.
6. **How much RabbitMQ coupling exists? Low at the consumer, significant inside the platform.** MQTT 5/TLS/QoS1/topic/schema are public. Queue names, native resource/topic ACLs, Management APIs, HTTP auth callbacks, classic DLX and session-to-queue mapping are internal implementation choices. OAuth re-authentication/shared-subscription/retained-store limitations affect the public feature set even when client code is standard.
7. **What happens offline? Demonstrated within short test bounds.** A prior QoS1 subscription and persistent session accumulate eligible events. Five events recovered in the reliability suite. E disconnected half of 12 consumers and recovered all 2400 expected deliveries; its measured post-publication recovery was about 120ms in that small run. Expired events are removed, and session expiry deletes bindings/state. No pre-subscription recovery or replay is provided. Long offline retention, backlog exhaustion and impacts at thousands of tenants remain unverified.
8. **What delivery guarantees can be offered? Protocol-level only.** QoS1 redelivery after an interruption before PUBACK and duplicate handling were demonstrated. Delivery is bounded by successful subscription, session/message expiry, queue limits and broker/consumer availability. There is no exactly-once, application processing receipt, durable sample deduplication across restarts or production HA guarantee. Ingress confirms, native DLX and the internal DLQ do not create an unconditional transactional delivery guarantee. External business failures are the integrator's responsibility.
9. **What telemetry fits Azure? Exportable, not integrated.** Aggregate Prometheus metrics, selected queue diagnostics, structured auth/onboarding/error logs, application latency histograms and runtime CPU/memory/disk snapshots are available. A future approved Azure Monitor managed Prometheus/Application Insights integration can reuse standard endpoints/OTel conventions. Avoid per-event/service labels and continuous full-object scraping. Azure export, collector deployment and cloud billing were not tested.
10. **What could be reused in production? Candidates, not production-ready components.** Versioned minimal schema, public MQTT contract, sample/DX material, identity/clientId binding, activation/read-back checks, negative security tests, lifecycle reconciliation approach, resource guards and benchmark methods. Replace the synthetic API key map, local JSON registry/plaintext issuance files and single-node secret/bootstrap arrangements before production. Do not reuse an application factory that initializes unrelated cloud clients.
11. **What needs further investigation/Design Review?** Authoritative message-to-service enrichment; APIM/IAM key verification, approval, rotation and revocation; durable provisioning transactions; registered-app quotas; per-service storage/backlog policy; persistent-queue capacity at thousands of services; steady-state/soak/open-loop load; auth outage/caching; clustered/quorum durability and DLX behavior; production TLS/DNS; resilient secrets and operator audit; reconnection storms; distributed-clock latency; a tested Java sample. Broker failover was explicitly excluded locally. Azure requires the user's requested new discovery/design round.
12. **What operational risks and costs precede adoption?** Always-on stateful broker/storage, per-session backlog and fanout duplication, auth/control-plane availability, certificate and credential lifecycle, orphan/session cleanup, poison handling, telemetry cardinality and data privacy, capacity limits/noisy neighbors, backups/upgrade procedures and operational responsibility for public sockets. Real currency/infrastructure cost is unknown and was not estimated as a measurement. A public event service requires security and capacity evidence beyond these local runs.

## Application-Like Scenario H

The sender service's real relationship to CreateMessages is represented synthetically, not integrated: one CMS-shaped serviceId/key/topic, several distinct messageId creates, and three correlated fixture events (ACCEPTED, PROCESSED, read=true) for each create. Production currently uses services-func; messages-app has no production traffic yet. Neither surface, Services CMS nor real IO queues/change feeds are called by this test.

H additionally demonstrates **1000 service-scoped topics, subscriptions, persistent sessions and queue bindings**, with **3000 complete message sequences and all 9000 expected deliveries**, zero observed isolation violations, two explicit negative subscription probes, and successful scoped cleanup. The generator's 1000-service online window was 250: these are not 1000 concurrent connections. Events pass through the actual AMQP ingress and PoC worker instead of A-G's direct MQTT publishing. Completion is checked for every message/version, without requiring ordered reception.

The earlier attempt with 1000 simultaneous consumers failed during onboarding before event publication; Docker became unreachable while Rancher still reported its VM started. Cause remains unverified, so no broker saturation conclusion is drawn. The 950 synthetic records of that interrupted run were cleaned up after an explicitly authorized runtime restart. All original failed evidence is preserved in [the report](mqtt-poc-benchmark.md#scenario-h-service-creates-and-correlated-events).

The A-G statements above refer to their original experiments. H extends the demonstrated scope to 1000 distinct service session queues and correlated message lifecycle delivery, but does not resolve sustained full-concurrency/HA/production IAM or real-source integration gaps. H is a fixture, not the complete IO transition graph; simulated read events do not imply all real messages are read. Offline-window latency is not comparable to direct-MQTT latency. No production SLO or maximum capacity is asserted.
